What Happens If You Die Without a Will? (Dying Intestate, Explained)

← Back to blog

If you die without a will, you don't get to skip the decision about who inherits your assets — the decision just isn't yours anymore. Every state has "intestate succession" laws that dictate exactly who receives what when someone dies without a valid will. They're formulaic, they don't know your family, and they frequently produce outcomes people never would have chosen.

How intestate succession generally works

While the specifics vary significantly by state, most intestate succession laws follow a similar general structure:

  • A surviving spouse typically receives a share of the estate — but often not all of it if there are also surviving children, especially children from a prior relationship.
  • Surviving children (including from prior relationships) generally receive a defined share, divided among them.
  • If there's no spouse or children, the estate typically passes to parents, then siblings, then more distant relatives, following a defined order.
  • If no relatives can be located at all, the estate can ultimately pass to the state — a rare outcome, but a real one.

Because rules vary by state and change over time, the exact percentages and order of priority for your situation depend on where you live. This is general information, not legal advice for your specific circumstances.

Who intestate succession leaves out completely

This is the part that surprises people most. Intestate succession laws only recognize legally defined relationships — they generally do not provide for:

  • Unmarried partners. No matter how long you've been together or how intertwined your lives are, an unmarried partner typically receives nothing under intestate succession.
  • Stepchildren you never legally adopted. Without adoption, stepchildren generally have no automatic inheritance right.
  • Friends, godchildren, or chosen family. Only legally recognized relatives inherit under intestate law — anyone outside that structure is excluded, regardless of your actual wishes.
  • Charities or causes you cared about. Intestate succession has no mechanism for charitable giving.

It's not just about who gets what

Dying without a will also means:

  • No say in guardianship. If you have minor children and no surviving parent, a court decides who raises them — without your input, unless you've documented a preference elsewhere.
  • A court-appointed administrator, rather than someone you chose, manages your estate — and that person may need to post a bond, adding cost and delay.
  • Probate either way. Intestate estates still go through probate — the public, months-long court process — so you don't avoid any of the downsides of a will; you just lose the ability to direct the outcome.
The core issue: intestate succession isn't a safety net — it's a default formula that applies whether or not it reflects what you would have actually wanted. The only way to make sure your wishes are followed is to document them.

The fix is simpler than people think

You don't need to resolve every hypothetical to avoid dying intestate — you need a validly executed will (and ideally a funded living trust) that names your beneficiaries, your executor, and a guardian for any minor children. That's it. The formal document, properly signed and witnessed according to your state's requirements, is what takes the decision out of the state's formula and puts it back in your hands.

What the intestate process actually looks like, step by step

When someone dies without a will, here's the general sequence courts follow:

  • Someone petitions the probate court to open an estate — usually a close relative, since there's no named executor to do it.
  • The court appoints an administrator (the intestate equivalent of an executor), following a state-defined priority order among surviving relatives. This person may be required to post a bond — an added cost that a named executor in a will can often waive.
  • The estate is inventoried — every asset identified and valued, a public process filed with the court.
  • Creditors are notified and given a window to make claims against the estate before anything is distributed.
  • Remaining assets are distributed according to the state's intestate succession formula, once debts and administration costs are settled.

This entire process — public, court-supervised, and following a fixed legal formula rather than your actual wishes — is what a will and trust are designed to shortcut or avoid entirely.

A common misunderstanding: "my spouse gets everything automatically"

Many people assume a surviving spouse simply inherits the entire estate by default. In most states, that's only guaranteed to be true in specific circumstances — often when there are no surviving children, or when all surviving children are also the children of that spouse. If there are children from a prior relationship, a surviving spouse frequently shares the estate with them under the state's formula, sometimes resulting in a spouse owning a home jointly with stepchildren they don't have a close relationship with. This is exactly the kind of counterintuitive outcome that a documented plan avoids.

Frequently asked

Does having a small estate mean intestate succession doesn't matter?

No — many states do offer a simplified, faster probate process for small estates below a certain value, but the underlying intestate succession formula for who inherits still applies. A small estate can still go to unintended relatives, or skip an unmarried partner entirely.

Can family members just agree among themselves instead of following the formula?

Sometimes, informally, if everyone entitled to inherit agrees to a different division and the court allows it — but this requires unanimous agreement among everyone with a legal claim, which is exactly the kind of negotiation a will is meant to make unnecessary in the first place.
