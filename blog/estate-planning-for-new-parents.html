Estate Planning for New Parents: What You Actually Need

← Back to blog
July 29, 2026·6 min read

Having a child is one of the few life events that makes estate planning genuinely urgent rather than "someday" — because for the first time, there's a question no one else can answer for you: who raises your child if you can't?

The document most new parents miss: guardian nomination

Without a legal guardian nomination, that decision goes to a family court — without any documented input from you. Relatives can end up disputing who's best suited, a process that plays out publicly and slowly, at the worst possible time for a child who has just lost both parents. A guardian nomination form lets you name your first choice, and ideally a backup, in your own words, in a legally recognized document.

This one document is arguably the single highest-priority item in a new parent's estate plan — more urgent, in practical terms, than decisions about who inherits the china.

Control how and when your child inherits

A basic will can direct that assets go "to my children," but on its own, that often means an 18-year-old receiving a lump sum the day they turn 18 — rarely the outcome parents actually want. A trust lets you set staged milestones instead: a portion at 25, more at 30, the remainder at 35, for example, or funds released for specific purposes like education first. You decide the terms; the trust enforces them.

Life insurance and beneficiary designations

Many new parents take out life insurance for the first time, but overlook that the policy's beneficiary designation controls where that payout goes — regardless of what your will says. If you name your minor child directly as beneficiary, insurers generally cannot pay a lump sum directly to a minor, which can force a court-supervised guardianship of the funds. Naming your trust as beneficiary instead lets the trust terms — the ones you actually chose — govern how and when your child receives the money.

Healthcare and financial authority — for you, not just your child

New parents often focus entirely on what happens to their child, and skip the documents that protect the family if a parent becomes incapacitated rather than deceased. A healthcare directive and medical power of attorney let someone you trust make medical decisions on your behalf if you can't. A durable power of attorney does the same for financial matters — paying the mortgage, accessing accounts, keeping the household running — while you recover.

A simple starting checklist

  • Guardian nomination form, naming a primary and backup guardian
  • A will or trust with staged distribution terms appropriate for a minor beneficiary
  • Updated life insurance beneficiary designations — naming the trust, not the child directly
  • Healthcare directive and medical power of attorney for both parents
  • Durable power of attorney for financial matters
  • A letter of guidance to the future guardian — not legally binding, but invaluable in practice: routines, values, medical history, what you'd want for this child
Don't let "we'll finish it eventually" become the plan. The guardian nomination alone takes minutes to complete and closes the single biggest gap most new parents have.
