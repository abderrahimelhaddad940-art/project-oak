Short answer: not necessarily. Longer answer: it depends on how complex your situation actually is — and most people overestimate their own complexity.

What an attorney actually does

When you hire an estate attorney, you're generally paying for three things: drafting documents customized to your situation, advice on strategy for anything unusual (tax exposure, business succession, a contested family situation), and review to make sure everything is internally consistent and properly executed. For a standard estate, that customization is often more billable hours than actual legal complexity — the underlying documents follow a well-established structure regardless of who drafts them.

Signs your situation is straightforward

  • You want to leave your assets to your spouse, children, or a small number of clearly named beneficiaries
  • You don't own a business with multiple owners or complex succession questions
  • Your estate is below the federal estate tax exemption threshold (a high bar for most households)
  • You aren't navigating a contested divorce, active litigation, or family conflict likely to result in a will challenge
  • You want standard provisions: a guardian for minor children, clear distribution instructions, healthcare and financial powers of attorney

If that describes you, a guided, professionally drafted template — the kind used as the foundation for most attorney-prepared plans anyway — will very likely get you a complete, valid plan.

Signs you should talk to an attorney

  • Blended families with complex dynamics. Second marriages, children from prior relationships, and unequal distributions can benefit from customized language beyond a standard template — though GuardTies does include second-marriage protection clauses for many common scenarios.
  • Business ownership with other partners. Succession planning that involves other owners, buy-sell agreements, or complex valuation questions.
  • Large or complicated estates. If your estate approaches the federal estate tax exemption, sophisticated tax strategy is worth a professional's time.
  • Active disputes. If you expect your will to be contested, an attorney's involvement adds a layer of documented, professional oversight.
  • Special needs beneficiaries. Leaving assets to someone receiving means-tested government benefits requires specific trust structures to avoid disqualifying them.

The real cost comparison

A comparable attorney-drafted estate plan typically runs $3,500–$10,000+, plus weeks of scheduling and back-and-forth. A guided, professionally drafted template package costs a fraction of that and can be completed in a weekend. The gap in outcome, for a standard estate, is much smaller than the gap in price and time.

The honest framework: the more standard your family and financial situation, the less a custom attorney engagement actually buys you. The more unusual your situation — business ownership, contested family dynamics, high net worth — the more that customization is worth paying for.

What "DIY" actually means in practice

DIY estate planning doesn't mean improvising legal language from scratch — it means completing professionally drafted documents rather than commissioning custom ones. The structural clauses, execution requirements, and legal language in a standard will or trust are well-established regardless of who fills them in. What changes between a $499 guided package and a $5,000 attorney engagement, for a standard estate, is mostly customization you may not need and the built-in consultation time you're paying for by the hour.

The risk with DIY isn't the document template itself — it's completing it incorrectly, applying the wrong documents to your situation, or executing it improperly (missing a required witness, skipping notarization). That's specifically what a good guided system is built to prevent: a needs assessment that tells you which documents apply, plain-English notes that explain each clause, and a state-specific execution guide that removes the guesswork from signing.

A middle path: hybrid review

You don't have to choose purely between "fully DIY" and "fully custom attorney engagement." Many people complete a guided template package themselves and then pay an attorney for a one-time review — a much shorter, cheaper engagement than a from-scratch drafting project, since the attorney is checking your completed documents rather than building them from a blank page. This is a reasonable option if your situation is close to standard but you want a professional's sign-off before you finalize things.

Frequently asked

Will a DIY will hold up in court?

A properly executed will — signed and witnessed according to your state's requirements — is legally valid regardless of whether an attorney drafted it or you completed a professionally drafted template. Legal validity depends on correct content and correct execution, not on who typed it.

What's the biggest risk of doing it yourself?

Improper execution — missing a witness, skipping notarization where required, or leaving a section incomplete — is a far more common failure point than the document's underlying language. That's why a state-specific execution guide matters as much as the documents themselves.
